How care teams use this: A care manager working through the top 5% flagged list (\~4,500 patients per cycle) will find roughly 44 per 100 who genuinely need outreach — 5.9× more productive than reviewing a random list of the same size.

Takeaway: At the recommended top-5% threshold, the August model flags \~4,500 patients per cycle with 44% PPV — improved from 39% in July. That means \~5 additional true high-risk patients identified per 100 reviewed, compared to the July version.

What are care gap indicators?

Twelve binary features capturing whether specific monitoring or care activities are absent from a patient’s record in the prior 22 months:

Type Examples
Lab monitoring gaps No A1C test, no eGFR, no lipid panel, no creatinine, no urine albumin
Procedure and education gaps No retinal exam, no foot exam, no diabetes education, no nutrition counseling
Aggregate measure Total count of gaps (0–11)

The 22-month window exceeds the standard 12-month screening cadence — a flag truly means a patient has fallen out of routine care.

Feature Model importance

No diabetes education in 22 months Top 10

Fewer than 8% of T2DM patients received nutrition counseling in the 22-month window. Fewer than 9% received documented diabetes education. These are among the most common gaps — and the most predictive.

Takeaway: Asking “has this patient received expected diabetes care?” turns out to be the single most powerful question for improving risk prediction — the largest gain from any single experiment in the project’s history.

Care Gaps — What the Data Actually Shows
SLIDE 5 OF 9

An Important Nuance: Sicker Patients Get MORE Monitoring

The raw data shows a counterintuitive pattern: patients who DO have gaps show lower admission rates — often dramatically so.

Care gap Admission rate (gap present) Admission rate (gap absent)
No nutrition counseling 5.5% 32.6%
No diabetes education 6.5% 17.5%
No A1C test 3.0% 9.7%
No creatinine test 1.3% 9.0%

This is confounding by indication: sicker patients receive more intensive monitoring precisely because they are higher-risk. Among nutrition counseling recipients, admitted and non-admitted patients have virtually identical monitoring gap burdens (3.8 vs 3.9 out of 11) — what separates them is prior severity: 2× prior admissions, 2× ED visits, +47% charges.

What the +0.031 improvement proves

If care-gap features were purely redundant with the 285 existing severity measures, they would add zero. The largest gain in the project’s history demonstrates they carry genuinely new information — but that information operates in multivariate interaction, not as a simple rule.

What this means for intervention design

  1. For risk prediction: These features capture a dimension of information not redundant with anything else in the model.
  2. For intervention: The care activities involved (nutrition counseling, diabetes education) can be offered to flagged patients regardless of the mechanism — they are directly actionable.

Takeaway: The relationship between care gaps and risk is real but complex. What matters for clinical teams: the model is measurably better with these features, and the interventions they suggest (offering missed services) are actionable regardless of the underlying mechanism.

CGM Data Correction — Transparent Reporting
SLIDE 6 OF 9

A Data Correction Revealed a New Clinical Signal — Insulin Pump Use

In July, we reported that CGM (continuous glucose monitor) use was a meaningful predictor of risk. That finding still stands — but we found and fixed a data-labeling issue that made it more accurate.

What happened

Two insulin pump device codes had been miscounted as CGM sensors. When we corrected the device list (counting only actual CGM sensors), model performance initially dropped — meaning the original miscoding had been quietly helping.

Rather than reverting to the incorrect coding, we investigated why the correction affected performance.

What we discovered

Insulin pump use is itself a meaningful, distinct clinical signal — separate from CGM use. The model had been picking it up indirectly through the miscoded devices.

Patient group Unplanned admission rate
Insulin pump users \~10 per 100
Non-pump users \~7 per 100

Patients on insulin pumps have roughly a third higher admission rates — reflecting genuine clinical complexity (more advanced or harder-to-manage diabetes), not a data artifact.

Resolution

Step Outcome
Corrected CGM device list (sensors only) CGM signal now clinically accurate
Built insulin pump use as its own separate feature New signal captured properly
Re-ran model with both clean signals Performance recovered and slightly exceeded July benchmark

Validation in progress: We are working with our Clarity team to formally validate the full insulin pump device/medication code list before permanent production use.

Takeaway: The July CGM finding stands. The correction simply made it more accurate, and revealed a second related signal (insulin pump use) that we are now formally incorporating. Transparent data correction led to a better model.

Care Utilization Patterns — Ready for September
SLIDE 7 OF 9

How Patients Use Care Now Has a Measurable Impact (Experiment B — Pass, Feature Retained)

Question: Beyond counting visits, does HOW patients engage with the system predict risk? Visit regularity, missed appointments, repeat ED use, and care fragmentation across departments.

What was built and tested

We engineered 14 care-pattern features from encounter, scheduling, and hospital records, then ran a full H2O DAI experiment alongside the existing 285-feature model.

Metric Before (benchmark) After (+ 14 care patterns) Change
PR-AUC (held-out test) 0.3962 0.3971 +0.0009
Total features 285 299 +14

The aggregate improvement is marginal. But the experiment revealed something significant about which individual features the model values.

The key finding: specialty fragmentation is a top-tier predictor

#2
overall model importance (of 299 features)

The number of distinct specialties a patient visits ranked #2 out of 299 features — second only to diagnostic complexity. This is the strongest result for any newly-added feature in the project’s history.

What this means clinically

  • Not just how much care a patient uses, but how scattered that care is across specialties
  • A known correlate of care coordination challenges — gaps in follow-up, conflicting plans, unclear ownership
  • Unlike diagnosis count (largely fixed), specialty fragmentation is potentially actionable through care coordination

Takeaway: n_distinct_specialties is permanently retained in the production model. Patients whose care is fragmented across many specialty areas are at substantially higher risk — and unlike disease burden, fragmentation is something care teams can directly address.

Baseline Feature Recovery — Negative Result (Closed)
SLIDE 8 OF 9

The Current Approach Isn’t Missing Signals From Earlier Work (Experiment C — Negative Result, Closed)

Question: An earlier model version used 854 features built differently from the same data. Are there useful signals in that older approach that the current 11-dimension architecture does not capture?

What we tested

Combined the current 285 features with 349 surviving legacy features (634 total) and ran the full modeling pipeline on the same cohort, same split, same settings.

Configuration Features Held-out test PR-AUC
Current model (benchmark) 285 0.396
Current + 349 legacy features 634 0.395

Adding the legacy features produced no improvement. The difference (−0.001) is within the model’s run-to-run variability (±0.011).

Why they didn’t help

The expanded model relied almost entirely on the same features the current model already uses. Legacy features (medication names, lab values, vital signs) are different column names for concepts the 11 curated clinical dimensions already capture.

What this means

This is a reassuring negative result. The model’s performance ceiling is limited by the inherent predictability of unplanned admissions from structured EHR data — not by missing information.

Takeaway: The current 11-dimension approach captures everything the older 854-feature model did, more efficiently and with better clinical interpretability. Future gains will come from genuinely new data concepts, not retrofit of legacy features.

Clinical verification

Before deployment, the model requires a patient chart review round:

  • Clinicians review a sample of high-risk flagged patients and confirm the model’s predictions align with clinical judgment
  • This is a standard validation step — it tests whether the statistical signal translates into clinically meaningful risk identification
  • The review also informs operating threshold selection: at what cutoff does the flagged list match care team capacity?

Production deployment — decisions needed from this group

Moving from a validated model to live scoring requires three things:

Component What it involves
Scoring infrastructure H2O DAI model packaged as a scoring pipeline — runs on a schedule against the live patient cohort, produces a ranked risk list
Prediction delivery Where do predictions land? Options include a standalone dashboard, integration into Epic (BPA/SmartList), or a staged approach (dashboard first, Epic later)
Ongoing maintenance Retraining cadence, monitoring for drift, and cost of upkeep (DS team will bring a structured estimate)

We need clinical input on prediction delivery. The DS team can build toward any of these endpoints, but the care management workflow should drive the choice — not the technology.
