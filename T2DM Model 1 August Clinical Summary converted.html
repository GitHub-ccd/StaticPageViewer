The Model Is a Prioritization Tool — Not a Diagnostic

Translating model performance into practical terms: of every 100 patients the model flags, how many will actually have an unplanned admission?

Targeting Group Patients Flagged Of every 100 flagged, how many admitted? All admissions captured Lift vs. random

Based on held-out test set: 90,087 patients, 6,730 admissions, 7.47% base rate.

How care teams use this: A care manager working through the top 5% flagged list (\~4,500 patients per cycle) will find roughly 44 per 100 who genuinely need outreach — 5.9× more productive than reviewing a random list of the same size.

Important framing: The model is a triage tool, not a diagnostic. Fewer than half of patients in the top 5% will have an admission — this is expected and appropriate. The goal is to focus scarce care management attention where it will have the most impact, not to achieve diagnostic certainty.

Takeaway: At the recommended top-5% threshold, the August model flags \~4,500 patients per cycle with 44% PPV — improved from 39% in July. That means \~5 additional true high-risk patients identified per 100 reviewed, compared to the July version.

Care Gap Indicators — The Biggest Gain in the Project (PASS)

Patients Missing Expected Diabetes Care Are the Strongest Signal of Risk ✅ (Experiment A — Pass, New Benchmark)

What was tested: We measured whether patients are NOT receiving expected diabetes monitoring — lab tests, eye exams, foot exams, nutrition counseling, and diabetes education — and asked the model whether these gaps help identify risk beyond what test results alone show.

This is the largest single improvement from any experiment in the project to date. The previous best single-experiment gain was +0.009 (CGM device identification, July).

What are care gap indicators?

Twelve binary features capturing whether specific monitoring or care activities are absent from a patient’s record in the prior 22 months:

Type Examples
Lab monitoring gaps No A1C test, no eGFR, no lipid panel, no creatinine, no urine albumin
Procedure and education gaps No retinal exam, no foot exam, no diabetes education, no nutrition counseling
Aggregate measure Total count of gaps (0–11)

The 22-month window exceeds the standard 12-month screening cadence — a flag truly means a patient has fallen out of routine care.

The most important predictor in the entire model is now “no nutrition counseling”

Feature Model importance
No nutrition counseling in 22 months #1 (most important in the entire model)
No diabetes education in 22 months Top 10
Total monitoring gaps (aggregate count) Top 20

Fewer than 8% of T2DM patients received nutrition counseling in the 22-month window. Fewer than 9% received documented diabetes education. These are among the most common gaps — and the most predictive.

An important nuance: sicker patients get MORE monitoring

At first glance, the pattern seems counterintuitive: patients who DO have gaps show lower admission rates in raw data. This is because sicker patients are seen more often and tested more frequently.

However, in the full model context (alongside lab values, diagnoses, medications), the care-gap features identify a specific population: patients who may be at risk but are not being actively monitored. These are patients the model would otherwise miss.

Why this matters for intervention design

This finding has a dual value:

  1. For risk prediction: The model now better identifies patients falling through the cracks of structured diabetes management.
  2. For intervention: The features that matter most (nutrition counseling, diabetes education) are precisely the services that can be offered to flagged patients as part of the outreach intervention.

Takeaway: Asking “is this patient receiving expected diabetes care?” turns out to be the single most powerful question for identifying unplanned admission risk. And unlike many risk factors, missing care is directly actionable — a flagged patient can be offered the services they are missing.

CGM Data Correction — Transparent Reporting
SLIDE 5 OF 7

A Data Correction Revealed a New Clinical Signal — Insulin Pump Use

In July, we reported that CGM (continuous glucose monitor) use was a meaningful predictor of risk. That finding still stands — but we found and fixed a data-labeling issue that made it more accurate.

What happened

Two insulin pump device codes had been miscounted as CGM sensors. When we corrected the device list (counting only actual CGM sensors), model performance initially dropped — meaning the original miscoding had been quietly helping.

Rather than reverting to the incorrect coding, we investigated why the correction affected performance.

What we discovered

Insulin pump use is itself a meaningful, distinct clinical signal — separate from CGM use. The model had been picking it up indirectly through the miscoded devices.

Patient group Unplanned admission rate
Insulin pump users \~10 per 100
Non-pump users \~7 per 100

Patients on insulin pumps have roughly a third higher admission rates — reflecting genuine clinical complexity (more advanced or harder-to-manage diabetes), not a data artifact.

Resolution

Step Outcome
Corrected CGM device list (sensors only) CGM signal now clinically accurate
Built insulin pump use as its own separate feature New signal captured properly
Re-ran model with both clean signals Performance recovered and slightly exceeded July benchmark

Validation in progress: We are working with our Clarity team to formally validate the full insulin pump device/medication code list before permanent production use.

Takeaway: The July CGM finding stands. The correction simply made it more accurate, and revealed a second related signal (insulin pump use) that we are now formally incorporating. Transparent data correction led to a better model.

Care Utilization Patterns — Ready for September
SLIDE 6 OF 7

How Patients Use Care Is Now Measurable — and Ready for the Next Experiment (Experiment B — Pass)

Question: Beyond counting visits, does HOW patients engage with the system predict risk? Visit regularity, missed appointments, repeat ED use, and care fragmentation across departments.

What we found

A comprehensive source review confirmed all five proposed feature areas are feasible with existing data — no new data sources required:

Feature area What it captures Cohort coverage
Visit spacing and regularity Is care consistent or erratic? 90%
No-show and cancellation behavior Is the patient disengaging? 72%
ED bounce-back (72-hour and 30-day) Repeat acute care use 26% with ED activity
Department and specialty fragmentation How spread across the system? 86%

Key finding: About 28% of T2DM patients had at least one no-show in the feature window. Department and specialty spread show strong variation, suggesting care fragmentation carries useful signal.

One feature already retained

When tested as part of the combined model, n_distinct_specialties (number of distinct specialties a patient has visited) ranked #2 in overall model importance — contributing a +0.001 PR-AUC gain on its own.

14 features built, validated, and ready

All 14 care-pattern features have been engineered, validated (300,290 rows, no nulls, all quality checks passed), and are staged for the September experiment cycle:

Category Features
Encounter patterns Visit spacing, variability, regularity, distinct specialties, fragmentation ratio, distinct providers
Scheduling behavior No-show count and rate, cancellation count and rate, left-without-being-seen, appointment lead time
ED revisit patterns 72-hour bounce-back count, 30-day revisit count

Takeaway: We now know not just how much care a patient uses, but how consistently they engage — and whether they miss planned care or return to acute care quickly after discharge. These patterns are ready to test in September and are clinically aligned with identifying patients whose care trajectory may be becoming unstable.
