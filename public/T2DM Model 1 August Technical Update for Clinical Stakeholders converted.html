CGM Device Users - Data Correction Story

2. CGM Device Users: A Data Correction and a New Finding

Why this matters

In our July review, we reported that patients using continuous glucose monitors (CGM) showed a meaningful lift in the model's ability to identify patients at risk of an unplanned admission. Since then, we took a closer look at exactly how that CGM signal was built, and found — and fixed — a data-labeling issue. Correcting it led to an unexpected and clinically useful discovery.

What we found

When we reviewed the medication codes used to identify CGM users in cp__order_med, two device codes turned out to be miscoded. CGM sensors and insulin pumps are different types of devices — a CGM sensor measures glucose; an insulin pump delivers insulin. Two codes for insulin pumps (specifically, pumps that read data from a CGM sensor as part of their operation) had been counted as CGM devices rather than as pumps.

Once we corrected the device list — counting CGM sensors only as CGM sensors — the model's performance on the held-out test set dropped noticeably. In other words, the original miscoding had been quietly helping the model, and simply fixing it looked, at first, like it hurt performance.

What we did about it

Rather than reverting to the incorrect device coding, we investigated why the correction affected performance. What we found is that insulin pump use is itself a meaningful, distinct clinical signal — separate from CGM use — that the model had been picking up indirectly through the miscoded devices. So we built insulin pump use into its own, separately tracked feature, so the model can use that information properly without it being mixed into the CGM signal.

CGM Device Users - Results and Clinical Interpretation

Result

Approach Held-out test performance
Original (July) — pump codes miscounted as CGM devices Benchmark
Corrected CGM device list, no separate pump signal Dropped notably below benchmark
Corrected CGM device list + a new, separate insulin pump signal Recovered, and slightly exceeded the July benchmark

Of every 100 patients flagged as high-risk by the updated model, roughly the same number go on to have an unplanned admission as with the July version (Positive Predictive Value is unchanged within noise). The correction did not weaken the tool — and once the insulin pump signal was properly separated out, the model performed slightly better than it did in July.

Clinical interpretation

Patients who use insulin pumps have unplanned admission rates roughly a third higher than patients who do not use pumps (about 10 of every 100 pump users vs. about 7 of every 100 non-pump users). This is independent of CGM use — a given patient may use a pump, a sensor, both, or neither — and reflects genuine clinical complexity (patients on pumps tend to have more advanced or harder-to-manage diabetes), not a data artifact.

CGM Device Users - Next Steps

Next steps

We are working with our Clarity team to formally validate the full list of insulin-pump device and medication codes before adding this signal as a permanent part of the model's feature set. Once that validation is complete, the corrected CGM signal and the new insulin pump signal will both become part of the standard model configuration used for scoring.

Key takeaway for stakeholders: the July CGM finding stands — CGM use remains a genuine, useful signal for this model. The correction simply made that signal more accurate, and revealed a second, related signal (insulin pump use) that we are now formally incorporating.

Experiment A: Monitoring and Care Gap Indicators

3. Experiment A: Monitoring and Care Gap Indicators

Why this experiment matters

Diabetes management relies on regular monitoring: lab tests, retinal exams, foot exams, diabetes education, and nutrition counseling. When these expected touchpoints are absent, it may indicate a patient who is disengaged from structured care or lost to follow-up. This experiment tests whether the absence of expected monitoring adds predictive value over and above the test results themselves.

The core question: does knowing that a patient has NOT received expected diabetes care help identify risk beyond what their lab values and visit history already tell us?

What was built

We created 12 binary indicators capturing whether specific monitoring or care activities are absent from the patient’s record in the 22 months prior to scoring. These divide into three groups:

Laboratory monitoring gaps (7 features)

Feature Definition
No A1C in 22 months Patient has no HbA1c result in the observation window
No eGFR in 22 months No estimated glomerular filtration rate test
No lipid panel in 22 months No cholesterol/triglycerides testing
No creatinine in 22 months No serum creatinine test
No urine albumin in 22 months No microalbumin or urine protein test
No TSH in 22 months No thyroid function test
No hepatic panel in 22 months No liver function tests

Procedure and education care gaps (4 features)

Feature Definition
No retinal exam in 22 months No dilated eye exam, OCT, or fundus photography
No foot exam in 22 months No diabetic foot assessment
No diabetes education in 22 months No structured diabetes self-management education
No nutrition counseling in 22 months No dietitian or nutrition counseling encounter

Aggregate measure (1 feature)

Feature Definition
Total monitoring gaps (0–11) Count of how many of the above 11 touchpoints are absent

The 22-month window was chosen to exceed the standard 12-month annual screening cadence, ensuring that a flag of 1 truly represents a gap rather than normal scheduling variation.

How common are these gaps?

Some gaps are very common in the cohort, while others are rare:

Care gap Percent of cohort with gap
No nutrition counseling 92.5%
No diabetes education 91.4%
No urine albumin test 81.7%
No eGFR test 74.1%
No foot exam 73.2%
No retinal exam 60.0%
No TSH test 45.0%
No A1C test 33.3%
No lipid panel 30.1%
No hepatic panel 21.9%
No creatinine test 19.9%

Notably, fewer than 10% of T2DM patients received nutrition counseling in the 22-month window, and fewer than 9% received documented diabetes education.

An important nuance in the data

At first glance, these features show a counterintuitive pattern: patients who DO have these gaps (no test, no exam) actually have a lower rate of unplanned admissions than those without gaps. This happens because sicker patients receive more intensive monitoring — they are seen more often and tested more frequently precisely because they are higher-risk.

However, the model handles this correctly. In the full multivariate context (alongside lab values, diagnoses, medications, and utilization data), the care-gap features help identify a specific population segment: patients who may be at-risk but are not being actively monitored. These are patients the model would otherwise miss.

Results

Configuration Features Held-out test PR-AUC
Current model (benchmark H17) 285 0.396
Current model + 12 care-gap indicators (XGBoost) 297 0.424
Current model + 12 care-gap indicators (H2O DAI) 297 0.427

This is the largest single improvement from any experiment in the project to date (delta +0.028 to +0.031). The previous best single-experiment gain was +0.009 from CGM device identification.

Both independent modeling engines (our internal XGBoost pipeline and H2O Driverless AI) confirm the improvement, reducing the chance this is a statistical artifact.

Which care gaps matter most?

The model identified nutrition counseling absence as the single most important feature in the entire expanded model — ahead of all 285 existing features. Diabetes education absence ranked in the top 10 by impact.

Feature Model importance ranking
No nutrition counseling in 22 months #1 (most important)
No diabetes education in 22 months Top 10
Total monitoring gaps (aggregate count) Top 20

Clinical interpretation

This finding has a dual interpretation:

  1. For risk prediction: Care-gap features capture a dimension of patient engagement and system-level care delivery that lab values and visit counts alone cannot represent. The model now better identifies patients who are falling through the cracks of structured diabetes management.

  2. For intervention design: The features that matter most (nutrition counseling, diabetes education) are precisely the ones that represent actionable care coordination opportunities. A patient flagged as high-risk partly because they have not received these services can be offered those services as part of the intervention.

What this means going forward

These 12 care-gap indicators will be incorporated into the production model configuration. The exact integration approach is being finalized. No additional data sources are required — all features are built from existing procedure and lab order records already in the feature pipeline.

Experiment B Overview

4. Experiment B: Care Utilization Patterns

Why this experiment matters

This experiment tests whether how patients use care adds predictive value beyond the current utilization features. The current model already captures basic counts and recency, such as prior ED visits, prior inpatient use, ambulatory encounter count, and days since last unplanned event. Experiment B explores whether more behavioral patterns can improve performance, including:

  • visit regularity versus irregular care use
  • missed appointment behavior
  • repeat ED use soon after discharge
  • fragmentation across departments and specialties

The core question is whether these patterns identify patients whose care trajectory is becoming unstable before an unplanned admission occurs.

What was explored

We completed a source review across Epic Clarity tables in the cp__ family under the refined layer to determine whether these concepts are actually measurable in the T2DM cohort.

The review focused on five proposed feature areas:

  1. Visit frequency and visit spacing patterns
  2. No-show rate
  3. ED bounce-back count
  4. Department fragmentation index
  5. Days-between-visits variability

This review was intentionally broad. Limiting the search to one encounter table would have missed important scheduling and ED-specific sources.

Main finding

All five proposed feature areas appear feasible with existing data sources.

A prior concern was whether appointment scheduling data would be available for no-show logic. That concern is now resolved. The scheduling source exists in the refined layer and contains appointment status fields needed to identify missed visits and cancellations.

In practical terms, this means Experiment B can move forward as a real feature-engineering experiment rather than staying in a concept stage.

Data Source Findings

Data source findings

Confirmed source coverage

The review identified three primary sources plus one department lookup table:

  • bsmh.refined.cp__pat_enc for encounter history and visit spacing
  • bsmh.refined.cp__f_sched_appt for appointment outcomes such as no-shows and cancellations
  • bsmh.refined.cp__pat_enc_hsp for ED revisit logic
  • bsmh.refined.cp__clarity_dep for department and specialty mapping

Cohort coverage observed during review

Source area What it supports Approximate cohort coverage
Encounter history visit spacing, utilization regularity, fragmentation 90.1%
Scheduling records no-show and cancellation behavior 72.1%
ED/hospital records ED revisit and bounce-back logic 25.9% with ED activity
Department mapping specialty and care-fragmentation views 85.7%

Additional observations

  • About 28% of the T2DM cohort had at least one no-show in the feature window.
  • ED return within 72 hours is present but relatively sparse, which makes it clinically meaningful but narrow.
  • Department and specialty spread show strong variation across patients, which suggests fragmentation may carry useful signal.

These findings support building a utilization-pattern layer that goes beyond simple counts.

Practical interpretation

The strongest message from this review is that the data support a broader view of utilization behavior:

  • not just how much care a patient used
  • but how consistently they engage
  • whether they miss planned care
  • and whether they return to acute care quickly after discharge

That combination is aligned with the clinical objective of identifying patients whose care patterns may indicate instability or breakdown in follow-up.

Proposed Feature Set and Plan

Proposed Experiment B feature set

Based on the source review, the planned feature set now includes the original concepts plus one additional ED pattern measure.

Encounter-pattern features

  • average time between visits
  • variability in time between visits
  • visit regularity measure
  • number of distinct specialties involved
  • specialty fragmentation ratio
  • number of distinct providers involved

Scheduling-behavior features

  • no-show count
  • no-show rate
  • cancellation count
  • cancellation rate
  • left-without-being-seen count
  • average appointment lead time

ED revisit features

  • 72-hour ED bounce-back count
  • 30-day ED revisit count

The 30-day ED revisit count was added because it captures a broader repeat-acute-care pattern and may be less sparse than the strict 72-hour bounce-back measure.

Recommended implementation path

Phase 1 — Complete

Source exploration is complete. We now have a clear understanding of where each concept should be built and which parts of the cohort are covered.

Phase 2 — Feature engineering build

Create a new care-patterns feature table in bsmh_ds_ph_wip with one row per patient and a consistent two-year lookback window ending at the cohort anchor date.

Phase 3 — Validation and data quality

Before model use, validate:

  • one row per patient
  • no unexpected nulls in engineered fields
  • feature ranges are clinically reasonable
  • event-based rates remain within expected bounds

Phase 4 — Model test

Run the care-pattern features as a standalone experiment and then add them into the combined model to assess whether they improve model discrimination.

Current recommendation

Experiment B is now justified as a real next-step feature engineering effort.

The review found enough usable data to proceed, and the feature set is more complete than originally expected. If the experiment performs well, these concepts can later be translated into a more detailed DS-facing technical reference and integrated into the broader project documentation.

Key observations for clinical context

  • Visit regularity varies widely. The coefficient of variation averaging 1.47 means that for the typical patient, the spread in visit timing is larger than the average gap itself. This may reflect patients who alternate between periods of engagement and periods of disconnection from care.

  • No-show behavior is concentrated. While 28% of the cohort has at least one no-show, the average rate is only 4%. A small group of patients accounts for the majority of missed appointments.

  • ED bounce-backs are sparse but clinically meaningful. Most patients never return to the ED within 72 hours. When they do, it often signals a pattern of care instability that the model may use to flag risk.

  • Fragmentation adds a new dimension. The current model captures how much care a patient uses. These features now tell the model how scattered that care is across providers and specialties — a known correlate of care coordination challenges.

Model experiment result

Status: H2O Driverless AI experiment complete (2026-08-08). Result: PASS — retained in production model.

Headline

Adding 14 care-pattern features to the existing 285-feature model produced a marginal improvement in overall discrimination:

Metric Before (benchmark) After (+ care patterns) Change
PR-AUC (held-out test) 0.3962 0.3971 +0.0009
ROC-AUC 0.852 0.852 unchanged
Total features 285 299 +14

The aggregate performance improvement is small and within statistical noise. However, the experiment revealed a significant finding about which individual features the model values.

The key finding: specialty fragmentation is a top-tier predictor

Of the 299 features in the combined model, the number of distinct specialties a patient visited ranked #2 overall in predictive importance — second only to the count of distinct clinical diagnosis groups, which has been the model’s top feature since its earliest versions.

This is the strongest result for any newly-added feature in the project’s history.

Rank Feature What it measures
1 N_DISTINCT_CLINICAL_GROUPS How many different diagnosis categories a patient has
2 n_distinct_specialties How many different specialty areas a patient is seen across
3 TOTAL_UNPLANNED_IP_PAST How many prior unplanned inpatient admissions

What this means clinically

Patients whose care is fragmented across many specialty areas are at substantially higher risk of unplanned admission — almost as predictive as the raw complexity of their diagnosis burden.

This is meaningful because:

  • It captures a dimension the model previously could not see: not just how much care a patient uses, but how scattered that care is.
  • Specialty fragmentation is a known correlate of care coordination challenges — patients seen by many different specialty teams may have gaps in follow-up, conflicting treatment plans, or unclear ownership of their overall care.
  • Unlike diagnosis count (which reflects disease burden and is largely fixed), specialty fragmentation is potentially actionable — care coordination interventions can reduce fragmentation for high-risk patients.

Why the other 13 features did not rank highly

The remaining care-pattern features (visit spacing, no-show rate, ED bounce-backs, etc.) did not appear in the top 27 features by model importance. This does not mean they carry zero signal — it means their predictive contribution is mostly already captured by existing features. For example:

  • Visit frequency patterns overlap substantially with encounter counts already in the model
  • ED revisit patterns overlap with prior IP/ED utilization features
  • No-show and cancellation rates may carry modest independent signal but at lower magnitude

Experiment C: Baseline Feature Recovery — Negative Result

5. Experiment C: Baseline Feature Recovery

Background

The current model uses 285 features organized across 11 clinical dimensions (demographics, medications, diagnoses, utilization, labs, social history, flowsheets, registry, surgical history, BMI, and glycemic markers). An earlier version of this model used a different approach with 854 features built from the same underlying data but assembled differently.

Experiment C asked: are there useful signals in the old 854-feature model that the current 11-dimension approach does not capture?

What we did

We compared the two feature sets column by column. Of the 1,071 columns in the legacy dataset, 349 survived initial quality filters (removing empty columns, near-constant values, and columns with less than 5% coverage). We then ran the full H2O DAI modeling pipeline on the current 285 features combined with the 349 legacy features (634 total) using the same patient cohort, the same train/test split, and the same modeling settings as our production benchmark.

Result

Configuration Features Held-out test PR-AUC
Current model (benchmark H17) 285 0.396
Current model + 349 legacy features 634 0.395

Adding the legacy features produced no improvement. The difference (−0.001) is well within the model’s run-to-run variability (±0.011).

Why the legacy features did not help

When we examined which features the expanded model relied on, the answer was clear: it relied almost entirely on the same features the current model already uses.

The top-ranked features in the combined model were:

  1. Number of distinct clinical diagnostic groups (current DX dimension)
  2. Illicit drug use indicator (current Social History dimension)
  3. Number of diagnostic sources used (current DX dimension)
  4. Prior unplanned inpatient admissions (current Utilization dimension)

The legacy features that scored highest (ranks 4–5) turned out to be TOTAL_UNPLANNED_IP_PAST — which is already in our current utilization dimension. The remaining legacy features (medication names, lab values, vital signs) are simply different column names for concepts the current model already captures through its 11 curated dimensions.

In short, the legacy model’s 854 features and the current model’s 285 features capture the same clinical information. The current approach does it more efficiently with better-organized, clinically interpretable features.

Clinical interpretation

This is a reassuring negative result. It means the current 11-dimension feature architecture is not missing important clinical signals that were present in earlier approaches. The model’s performance ceiling is not limited by missing data — it is limited by the inherent predictability of unplanned admissions from structured EHR data alone.

What this means going forward

We can confidently close this line of investigation. Future model improvements should focus on:

  • New data concepts not yet in the model (e.g., care utilization patterns from Experiment B)
  • Better representations of existing concepts (e.g., missingness indicators from Experiment A)
  • External data sources not currently in the EHR feature set

Retrofit of legacy features is not a productive path.
