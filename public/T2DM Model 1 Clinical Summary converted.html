What the Model Does in Practice

The Model Is a Prioritization Tool — Not a Diagnostic

Based on held-out test set: 90,087 patients, 6,730 admissions, 7.47% base rate.

How care teams use this: A care manager working through the top 5% flagged list (~4,500 patients per cycle) will find roughly 44 per 100 who genuinely need outreach — 5.8× more productive than reviewing a random list of the same size.

Important framing: The model is a triage tool, not a diagnostic. Fewer than half of patients in the top 5% will have an admission — this is expected and appropriate. The goal is to focus scarce care management attention where it will have the most impact, not to achieve diagnostic certainty.

Setting the Operating Threshold — How PPV and Sensitivity Guide Care Management Deployment

The model outputs a risk probability for every patient. The operating threshold determines how far down the ranked list your care management team reviews each cycle. It sets the fundamental tradeoff between two complementary metrics:

  • PPV answers: “Of the patients we flag, what fraction will actually be admitted?” — A higher threshold means fewer patients flagged, higher PPV, less wasted outreach effort.
  • Sensitivity answers: “Of all patients who will be admitted, what fraction did we catch?” — A lower threshold means more patients flagged, higher Sensitivity, fewer high-risk patients missed.

These cannot both be maximised simultaneously. The right operating point is determined by care management team capacity and programme goals.

Operating Strategy Patients per cycle PPV — of 100 flagged, how many admitted? Sensitivity — admissions captured
High-confidence (top 1%) ~900 ~64% ~9%
Balanced start (top 5%) ← Recommended ~4,500 ~40% ~29%
Broad outreach (top 10%) ~9,000 ~30% ~43%
High-volume (top 20%) ~18,000 ~24% ~65%

The CGM improvement makes every threshold more efficient. At the balanced starting point (top 5%), PPV improved from ~36.5% → ~39.2% — approximately 3 fewer unnecessary outreach contacts per 100 patients reviewed. At a cycle volume of 4,500 patients, that is ~135 fewer wasted contacts per cycle.

For deployment planning: Start at the top-5% threshold (~4,500 patients/cycle, ~40% PPV, ~29% Sensitivity). After one review cycle, calibrate based on actual team capacity and the observed value of each prevented admission. The threshold can be adjusted without changing the model.

Completed Experiment: Market-Specific Model Pilot

One Universal Model Works Equally Well in All Markets — No Separate Model Needed

Question raised at the June 23 review: Would training the model exclusively on our highest-prevalence markets produce better predictions for those populations?

Youngstown, Toledo Metro, Lima, and Springfield show unplanned admission rates of 11.1–12.3% — roughly 1.5× the cohort average of 7.47%. The hypothesis was that a model trained on this subpopulation would be more precisely calibrated to their risk patterns.

Test design: Two independent validation approaches were evaluated on 64,159 patients from the four markets and compared against results on the full 300,290-patient cohort. Both reached the same conclusion.

Experiments, Results, and Decision

Result: Full-Cohort Model Is More Accurate on Both Methods

Full Cohort (300K patients) 4-Market Subset (64K patients)
C-statistic 0.847 0.805 ↓
PR-AUC 0.387 0.420 †

An independent validation approach confirmed the same C-statistic decline (0.847 → 0.805).

† The higher PR-AUC in the subset reflects that these markets simply have more admissions per 100 patients — not that the model ranks patients better. C-statistic is the correct comparison: it measures ranking accuracy and is unaffected by prevalence.

Ranking accuracy (C-statistic) fell by 0.042 when the model was restricted to the four markets — confirmed by both validation approaches. A care manager using the market-only model would receive a less reliable priority list.

Takeaway: The full-cohort model identifies high-risk patients with equal skill in every market. A separate regional model would be less accurate and add engineering complexity. Decision: No market-specific model planned.

Experiment: Lab Trajectory Strategy 1 — CLOSED

Lab Trend Features Added No New Signal — The Model Already Extracts the Available Information (Strategy 1 — Closed)

Question: Can we improve predictions by tracking how lab values change over time, rather than capturing only the most recent value?

What was tested: 30 new features tracking the trend direction, peak, trough, number of readings, and variability over 22 months for five labs: eGFR, Creatinine, Anion Gap, Hemoglobin, and Troponin HS.

PR-AUC vs Benchmark
Benchmark (H2O DAI, all-11) 0.387 —
+ 30 Lab Trajectory features 0.384 −0.003

None of the 30 trajectory features ranked among the top 50 predictors. Two factors explain why:

  • Data too sparse for reliable trends: 73% of patients are missing eGFR readings; 88% are missing Troponin HS. A trend calculated from one or two values is noise, not a trend.
  • The snapshot already carries the signal: For better-covered labs (Creatinine 65%, Anion Gap 61%), a currently low or elevated value already reflects prior deterioration. The trajectory adds nothing beyond what the existing snapshot communicates.

Takeaway: The current lab features are at the practical ceiling of what the available data can support. No changes were made to the core feature tables. Strategy closed with no regression.

Experiment: Milliman Target Augmentation (Strategy 6) — CLOSED

Correcting Out-of-Network Labels Made Predictions Slightly Worse — and Confirmed Our Target Definition Is Right (Strategy 6 — Closed)

Question: Are we missing patients admitted outside of Bon Secours? Could fixing those mislabelled cases improve the model?

What was tested: We matched our T2DM cohort to Milliman Med Insight claims data (using Epic ID + date of birth) and identified 7,854 patients (2.6%) who had unplanned admissions recorded in Milliman but NOT in Epic. We created a corrected target label that counts these as positive events, raising the positive rate from 7.48% to 10.09%.

Approach Test PR-AUC vs Current Best
Current best (H2O DAI, original target) 0.387 —
All-11 + Milliman-corrected labels 0.381 −0.006

Result: Slight regression. The corrected labels did not improve — and slightly worsened — model performance. The top predictive features were unchanged from the current model.

Adding “correct” labels made the model slightly worse. The reason is counterintuitive but clear once we examine what these patients look like in our data.

The Milliman-only patients don’t look sick in our Epic records:

What the model sees Epic Positives Milliman-Only Non-admitted
Prior admissions in Epic High (0.97) Very low (0.16) Very low (0.13)
Charges in our system ~$92K ~$20K ~$15K
Diagnosis complexity High Moderate Low-moderate

These patients receive their acute care at other hospitals. In Epic, they appear low-risk — their complexity and utilization are captured elsewhere. Teaching the model that “patients who look healthy in our data are actually high-risk” actively confuses its decision boundary.

Takeaway: The Epic-only target is the right scope for BSMH care management. We can only intervene on patients whose care we coordinate. The model correctly predicts the admissions that are actionable within our system. Decision: Target definition unchanged.
