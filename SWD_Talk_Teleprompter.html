Now the framework itself. Six moves, done in order. These aren't optional, and the order matters. Most bad decks skip the first two — they go straight to making charts.

Understand the context. Storyboard before you build. Choose the right display. Eliminate clutter. Direct attention. And tell a story on every slide.

You'll notice this slide follows its own rule: only the first two moves are in color, because that's the point of the slide. Let's take them one at a time.

Move 1: before you write a single notebook cell, answer three questions.

WHO is the audience? For us, clinical leadership. They know the problem clinically, not statistically.

WHAT do you need them to do? Approve the next experiment phase — and trust the model.

HOW will they consume it? A live 30-minute review, and the slides also have to stand alone afterward.

For this room, think of this as the requirements spec. You wouldn't write code without knowing the requirement. This is the requirement for the deck.

Then you write the Big Idea, from a simple template: the finding, does something specific, which means a concrete impact.

"The T2DM model identifies high-risk patients 5.9 times more efficiently than random outreach, enabling targeted care management that could prevent thousands of unplanned admissions per cycle."

And the rule is strict: every slide must serve the Big Idea, or it gets cut.

Move 2: draft the story arc as a bulleted outline before any code or chart cells.

Setup is what is. Tension is what's wrong or unknown. Resolution is what we found, and what to do about it.

Here's the July storyboard. Why this cohort matters. Then good news first — a new feature based on continuous glucose monitoring gave the first benchmark gain since June. Then what that means operationally. Then credibility — two experiments were closed because they didn't help. Then where the highest-risk patients are. Then what comes next before we lock the model.

Compare that to June, which followed the technical order: team, cohort, architecture, findings, limitations.

July's logic was improvement → application → trust → next steps. It was sequenced so a clinician could follow it — not so a reviewer could audit it.

Move 3: the simplest display that shows the finding. Never pick a chart because it looks sophisticated.

Every row here is something we actually used. The 5.9× was a large text callout — no chart at all. Markets were a sorted horizontal bar. The benchmark over time was a line. Operating points were a table. Experiment outcomes were side-by-side cards.

And some things are simply never used: 3D charts, dual axes, pie charts with more than five slices, donuts, radar charts. If you catch yourself reaching for one, stop and ask what story you're actually trying to tell.

Move 4: clutter is anything that uses visual space without adding information — and most default chart settings are clutter.

Borders and heavy gridlines go. Tick marks and repeated axis titles go. Legends go — label the series directly. Extra decimals go: 7.5%, not 7.47%. Rainbow palettes go.

The test is simple: if you removed this element and the chart still says the same thing, remove it.

Move 5: if everything is highlighted, nothing is.

Color, bold, size, and position are processed by the eye before conscious thought — they're called preattentive attributes. The rule is to use exactly one primary attribute per chart. Everything else gets gray.

We fixed the palette for the whole project. Blue for structure. Green for a passed experiment. Red for a closed experiment. Orange for alerts and reference lines. And gray is the default — color is reserved for exactly what you want the audience to remember.

This deck follows the same rule. When we get to the translation table in a few minutes, watch where your eye goes — only one row is blue.

Move 6: every slide tells one story.

The pattern: an active-voice title that states the finding. One or two sentences of plain-language summary. One chart, table, or callout. And a closing takeaway.

"Model Performance" became "The model identifies high-risk patients 5.9 times better than chance." "Lab Trajectory Results" became "Snapshot labs already capture the information — trajectories add nothing."

Every title has to pass the "so what" test: if the audience remembers only one sentence from this slide, which sentence should it be? That sentence is the title. If you can't write it, the slide isn't ready.

In clinical ML, the presentation IS the product — at least until deployment. Clinicians decide whether to act on a model based on whether they trust the team presenting it.

Transparency about failures — closed experiments are shown, not buried. Clinical face validity — the top features make medical sense. Operational translation — their workflow, not our metrics. Appropriate humility — triage tool, not diagnostic. And good-news-first sequencing — story structure, not spin.

Two vocabulary rules are now hard-coded into every clinical document.

Rule one: name the production model, never the benchmarking tool. The production model is built in H2O Driverless AI. We use XGBoost internally for benchmarking. If a clinical document mentions both, people start asking which model is the real one.

Rule two: say PPV and sensitivity, never precision and recall. I know that one feels backwards here, because precision and recall are our words. They mean exactly the same things. But clinicians trained in epidemiology already know PPV and sensitivity — to them, precision and recall are just machine-learning jargon.

Both rules came out of real experience on this project, and both are written into the workflow, not left to memory.

Which raises the question: how do you make rules like these — and the whole framework — happen automatically every month?

When the assistant loads it, every design decision is already specified — title style, color, sequencing, translation — before the first cell is written. So the output is consistent, and it doesn't depend on me remembering every rule on a busy day.

It's a style guide that the tool actually reads and follows — rather than one that sits on a wiki.

August gave us the hardest test of that — so I'll walk you through it the way the August deck walked clinicians through it: three slides, setup, tension, resolution.

The August care-gap result was the hardest storytelling challenge of the project. A finding that contradicts intuition needs the full setup, tension, and resolution arc.

The care-gap features gave the project its largest single gain — plus 0.031 in PR-AUC, to 0.427 — and "no nutrition counseling in 22 months" became the number-one predictor in the whole model.

So you'd expect: patients missing expected diabetes care get admitted more often.

Now look. Patients with the gap — no nutrition counseling — have an admission rate of 5.5%. Patients who did get counseling: 32.6%. The exact opposite of intuition.

And it's not one odd feature. Diabetes education, A1C testing, creatinine testing — same direction every time.

Read literally, this says skip the counseling and patients do better. That's wrong — and if the story stops here, someone in the room will draw exactly that conclusion.

This is confounding by indication. Sicker patients get more care. Among patients who received counseling, the admitted and non-admitted groups had almost identical gap burdens — 3.8 versus 3.9 out of 11.

What separated them was prior severity: twice the prior admissions, twice the ED visits, and 47% higher charges.

And the +0.031 still matters: if these features were purely redundant with existing severity measures, they would add nothing. They carry new information — it just isn't a simple rule.

So we stated the guardrail explicitly to clinicians: the model uses care gaps to identify who is at risk, not to prescribe what to do about it. This is prioritization, not prescription.

A feature can be strongly predictive precisely because it's a proxy for severity. If you don't tell that story clearly, someone in the room will draw a causal conclusion.

Five lessons.

One: framework plus automation equals consistency. Quality no longer depends on remembering the rules.

Two: good news first isn't spin. It's structure that helps people remember.

Three: translation moved stakeholders more than any chart redesign. Turning "precision of 43.6%" into "of every 100 flagged, 44 will be admitted" was the highest-leverage change of the whole project.

Four: closed experiments build trust. Showing what failed raised our credibility.

Five: a generated PowerPoint means no drift between the notebook and the deck.
